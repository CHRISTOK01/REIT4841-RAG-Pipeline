# Manual check of the relevance labels

Two random samples of 30 query/chunk pairs from the curated KB (seed 30, drawn by
`python -m scripts.sample_relevance_pairs`), read one by one.

**Who judged:** the `Claude` column is the AI assistant's reading of each pair, made while
auditing the pipeline. It is one reader's opinion, not a clinician's. The `You` column is
blank for your own judgement; for the thesis, your labels are the ones to report.

Scale: **yes** = the chunk helps answer the question; **partly** = right condition and question
but peripheral, or only part of the chunk is useful; **no** = it does not help.

## What the samples show

- **Relevant-labelled chunks:** 20 of 30 clearly help, 5 partly, 5 do not.
  The 'no' cases are not random noise: they are boilerplate inside long MedQuAD answers (the GARD
  paragraph on how symptom frequencies are reported, lists of link titles, generic follow-up text).
  Because every chunk of the query's own answer is labelled relevant, such chunks inflate the number
  of relevant chunks a query has, which lowers Recall@k and NDCG@k for every retriever alike.
- **Retrieved-but-unlabelled chunks:** 0 of 30 clearly help, 3 partly, 27 do not.
  The labels are therefore missing few helpful chunks. Where a retriever is marked wrong it has
  usually returned a different condition, or the right condition but a different question.
- **So:** the strict labels are trustworthy as a floor for 'did retrieval find the answer' (MRR, and
  whether any gold chunk is in the top k), and less trustworthy for recall-style counts, which are
  depressed by non-informative 'relevant' chunks. With 30 pairs per sample the percentages carry a
  margin of roughly +/-15 points.
